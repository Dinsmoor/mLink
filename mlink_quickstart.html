Chapter 1

What mLink is

mLink is a complete radio messaging system for organised communications: an HF data network, a mail system and a net-control tool in one package. It turns an SSB transceiver, a sound interface and a computer into a station that moves messages and mail directly, through other stations, or through the internet, whichever works.

  • Nets with accountability. A named net with a net control, a roster of up to 127 stations, and a roll call that takes twelve stations about 21 seconds. Bulletins come back with a record of who received them. Standing orders are kept by every member. If net control drops out, a member takes over (Chapter 9).
  • Mail to anywhere in the world. Any station with internet access is a gateway to a central mailserver. A station on one frequency can send mail to a station on a different frequency or band, in another region or another country, or to an ordinary email address. Mail waits until it is collected and never expires (Chapter 8).
  • Still works when the internet is gone. Stations exchange messages and mail by radio alone. When the recipient cannot hear the sender, a station that hears both delivers the message without being asked (Chapter 7).
  • Several exchanges on one frequency at once. Traffic does not queue behind one two-station session. A long transfer, several short exchanges and a distress call can be on the air together (Chapter 6).
  • Strong encryption, for services that may use it. AES-256-GCM, with keys agreed between two stations from signed certificates or shared across a group. It is for services such as MARS and SHARES whose rules allow it, and stays off on amateur frequencies. On every service, a mail carries proof of its author that the mailserver checks (Section 12.5).
  • Strong compression. Every message is compressed with Zstandard at its highest setting, using a dictionary trained on radio traffic so that even a one-line message shrinks. Less to send means less time on the air and a better chance of getting through.
  • A distress call that gets through. A dedicated signal that decodes far below anything else on the frequency, which every station always listens for and passes on (Chapter 10).
  • One station, several radios, several operators. Computers on different bands join into one station under one callsign. Operators sign in with their own accounts and roles, and a mail can record who wrote it and from which position (Chapter 11).
  • A shared picture. Station positions, tracks, search areas and routes on every operator's map, and weather or other internet data fetched for stations that have no internet (Chapter 12).
Chapter 2

One system, top to bottom

mLink is designed as a whole, from the modem on the air to the mailbox on the internet, and is intended as a full replacement for the Winlink network and its several separate programs: the mail client, the modem, the gateway software and the central mail servers. Because every layer is built for the others, nothing has to be configured to match, and no air time is spent on negotiation between them.

  • The station daemon runs the station: the modems, the receiver, the database and every decision about the air. It keeps working with nobody watching, so a station can be left unattended to relay and to carry mail.
  • The client is what operators use: a desktop program or a web page. It can be on another computer, and several operators can work one station at once.
  • The mailserver keeps a mailbox for each callsign on the internet and is central to getting mail delivered: it is where mail waits for a station that is off the air, out of range or on another frequency.

mLink is built to beat Pactor, VARA, ARDOP and packet where it counts: mail that arrives, and a frequency that carries everyone's traffic. With those modems an operator keeps a list of gateways, picks one, and hopes it is listening and in range. An mLink network works together: every station is always decoding and always helping, so there is no particular gateway to reach. Several exchanges run at once, there is no session to set up, only what was lost is sent again, weaker stations are reached, and hard compression can put mLink ahead of even the fastest of them for ordinary text. A simulated twelve-station net running for hours delivered every mail and every message.

Chapter 3

The signal

An mLink channel is one SSB voice channel, about 3 kHz wide. Inside it are three kinds of signal, each in its own strip of the audio, each with its own modem. A receiver decodes all of them all the time.

StripAudioCarriesSounds like
Emergency125–175 HzDistress calls onlyA low, slow warble
Announce, three channelscentred on 400, 800 and 1,200 HzStation identification, short messages, acknowledgments, requests and repliesShort buzzy bursts, two thirds of a second each
Bulk1,500–3,000 HzLong messages, mail and attachmentsA wide, even hiss, 5.44 seconds per frame

A fourth announce-type channel sits in the middle of the bulk strip, at 2,250 Hz. It carries the short frames that open and acknowledge a bulk transfer, and takes turns with the bulk signal.

The strips are separated by empty spectrum, and every modem filters its own output, so a signal in one strip does not disturb another. Your radio's filters must pass audio from about 115 Hz to about 2,900 Hz, which is lower at the bottom than many voice settings.

Chapter 4

Modulation and error correction

Each strip has a modem chosen for its job. This page says what they are; the full manual explains how they work.

Signal levels are signal-to-noise ratios measured in a 3 kHz bandwidth. A report from FT8 or JS8 for the same signal reads about 0.8 dB higher.

  • The slower the signal, the deeper it reaches. A distress call decodes 12 dB below the short frames and 20 dB below the fast ones.
  • Reed-Solomon repairs up to 6 damaged bytes of the 23 in a short frame. A short frame fades out gradually: 99.7 % decode at −5 dB and 95 % at −6 dB.
  • LDPC works close to the theoretical limit and has a sharp edge: a bulk frame decodes perfectly or not at all. The station sends bulk only when it has measured the path at +6 dB or better.
  • A bulk frame is the same length at every rate. A better path carries more bytes per frame, not shorter frames. The first frame of a transfer is always rate 1/2.
  • Everything is compressed first when that makes it smaller, and every message carries a 16-bit check so a damaged one is never delivered.

Chapter 5

How a message is sent

You never choose how a message goes out. The station picks from the size of the message and from how well the other station has been hearing you.

Size after compressionSent asAir time
Up to 156 bytesA shortburst: up to 16 announce frames in a row2 to 12 s
157 to 601 bytes, weak pathA longburst: two to four shortbursts, each acknowledged15 to 47 s
100 bytes and up, good pathA bulk transfer: up to 10 bulk frames per transmission, 64 in all7 s for one frame, 56 s for ten

The largest message to one station is about 64,000 bytes after compression; a broadcast to everyone is limited to about 6,000.

5.1 One exchange

  1. The station waits until it is not in another exchange, nobody has reserved the part of the passband it needs, and its receiver hears nothing. Then it waits a short random time, so stations that were all waiting do not key together.
  2. It sends one transmission. The first frame, PRESENCE, is the station's callsign and grid square, which is also its identification. The next says what follows and for whom. Then come the data frames.
  3. Stations that hear the opening stay off that part of the passband until the transfer is over.
  4. The recipient replies about two thirds of a second after the transmission ends: an acknowledgment if everything arrived, or a map of which frames did not.
  5. The sender repeats only the missing frames. A fade that destroys one frame in ten costs one frame, not the transfer.

If no reply comes, the sender does not repeat the data. It asks one short question, "did you get that?", and if that is not answered either it looks for a relay (Chapter 7).

Full manual: "Mail in mLink", "Message sizes", "Reliable delivery".

Chapter 6

Several conversations at once

Most data modes give the whole channel to one transmission at a time. mLink divides the channel, so stations that are not talking to each other do not have to wait for each other.

  • Five places to transmit. Three announce channels, the bulk strip and the emergency strip. A bulk transfer between two stations can run in the top of the passband while other stations exchange short messages on the announce channels and a distress call goes out underneath.
  • Every receiver decodes all of it, all the time. A station is never tuned to one conversation. It hears every frame in every strip, which is how it knows who is on the frequency, how strong they are and what has been sent.
  • Reservations cover one strip. The opening of a transfer reserves only the announce channel or the bulk strip it will use, for only as long as the transfer can last. The other strips stay free.
  • A new message picks any free announce channel at random, so two stations that start at the same moment usually land on different channels and both get through.
  • Listen before transmitting. A station does not key while its receiver hears a signal, and after the frequency goes quiet it waits a random time that grows with the number of stations it has heard.
  • Order of precedence: distress traffic, replies that are owed, the exchange already under way, new messages, then automatic traffic such as beacons.

One station still transmits one thing at a time and cannot receive while it transmits. The gain is across stations: on a busy frequency several exchanges proceed side by side. It works: in a simulated net of twelve stations running for hours, not one frame was overlapped by another station's transmission. Even in a deliberately punishing search-and-rescue test, with two bands and many stations that cannot hear one another at all, 97.8 % or more of frames went out in the clear.

Full manual: "The passband and its regions", "Sharing the channel".

Chapter 7

Automatic relay

On HF a station often cannot hear the one it wants, while a third station hears both. mLink uses that third station without anyone asking it to.

  1. Every station keeps what it overhears. A message sent to someone else is held for a while by each station that decoded it.
  2. The sender asks. When a message gets no reply, the sender transmits one short REQUEST naming the recipient and the message. It does not send the message again.
  3. Stations that can help offer. A station offers only if it holds a copy, has heard the recipient in the last two minutes, and has a usable path to it. Offers go out in separate time slots so they do not collide, and each says how well that station hears the recipient. When many stations could help, each works out its own place in line, two answer per slot, and normally only the best two answer at all. A station far down the line stays silent and moves up each time a request goes unanswered, so that a crowd does not all answer at once.
  4. The sender picks one, two seconds after the first good offer: the station with the best path to the recipient.
  5. The relay delivers its copy in a transfer of its own, with repairs if needed. The recipient files the message under its original author.
  6. The relay reports back, and the sender's outbox shows the message as sent.

If nobody offers, the sender keeps the message and tries again when it next hears any station, first after one minute and then at gaps that double up to 32 minutes. Later requests also invite stations that hear the recipient but hold no copy; the sender hands one of them the message.

No routes are configured and no station is a designated relay. Each relay is arranged for one message, from what stations have heard in the last few minutes.

Full manual: "Relay", "Finding each other".

Chapter 8

Mail

Mail in mLink looks like email: a subject, recipients, copies, replies tied to the mail they answer, and attachments. You write it, send it and walk away.

8.1 How it travels

  • By radio, station to station. Directly to the recipient, or through a relay. No internet is involved at either end.
  • Through a gateway. A station with internet access says so on the air and becomes a gateway for the stations around it. It puts mail it overhears into the recipient's mailbox on the mailserver, and tells the sender it has taken it.
  • Across frequencies, bands and continents. Two stations do not have to share a frequency. Each needs only a gateway in radio range, or internet access of its own: the mail goes by radio to a gateway, over the internet to the mailserver, and out by radio from whichever gateway next hears the recipient, anywhere in the world.
  • To and from ordinary email. The mailserver passes mail between callsign mailboxes and internet email addresses, and runs mailing lists.

8.2 How it arrives

You do not have to ask. When a gateway hears your station, it looks in your mailbox and tells your station what is waiting; your station fetches it. Several mails for one station travel together in one transfer.

8.3 What to know

  • Mail never expires. A mail that cannot be delivered now waits, at your station or in the mailbox, until its recipient turns up.
  • One station is responsible for a mail at a time. A gateway claims a mail only after the mailserver has confirmed it holds it, and other stations carrying the same mail then stop.
  • A mail fits one transfer: about 64,000 bytes compressed to one station.
  • The mailserver is what makes delivery dependable. It is where mail waits, and every gateway reaches it. Stations with no internet in reach still exchange mail by radio alone; that is a feature for when the internet is gone, not the usual path.
  • Nothing secret is sent by radio. A station proves who it is to the mailserver without transmitting a password.
  • Encrypted mail skips the mailserver. For services whose rules allow encryption, an encrypted mail goes over the air to its addressees only. No gateway deposits it and no station carries it, so nothing holds it for a station that is off the air. That is today's limit: help from the mailserver and from stations that cannot read the mail is planned, not built.
  • The mailserver is an early version. Today it is one server with no management tools beyond ordinary SQLite database tools. Stronger security, spreading mailboxes over several servers, automatic duplication and management tools are planned, not yet built.

Full manual: "Mail over the air", "The mailserver".

Chapter 9

Named nets

Normally an mLink frequency has no net control. An operator who wants a net in the familiar sense can open one, give it a name, and let other stations join. Stations that do not join carry on as before on the same frequency.

  • Owner and net control. The net belongs to the station that opened it. Net control runs it. They are usually the same station, and the owner can hand net control to another member.
  • Roster. Up to 127 members, numbered in the order they joined. Net control repeats the roster a few entries at a time, so a station that arrives late learns it by listening.
  • Roll call. Net control sends one frame. Each member answers in its own 1.2-second slot with a single frame, in roster order, with no prompting. Twelve stations take about 21 seconds. A member that missed its slot gets a second try, and a member can vouch for one it hears that net control does not.
  • Bulletins. Up to 200 characters to every member, followed by a roll that shows who received it, and one repeat for those who did not.
  • Standing orders. One longer text that every member keeps. A member that does not have the current copy asks for it.
  • If net control goes quiet, a member takes over as a stand-in after three and a half cycles of silence, and the owner gets the net back when it returns.
  • During a roll call members hold their routine traffic, and stations outside the net keep off that one announce channel. The other channels and the bulk strip stay in use.

A net is a layer of organisation, not a requirement. Messages, mail, relay and distress calls work the same with or without one.

Full manual: "Named nets".

Chapter 10

Distress calls

Any station can send a distress call, and every station is always listening for one. The call has its own strip at the bottom of the passband and its own modem, built to be heard when nothing else can be.

  • It reaches deepest. A distress call decodes at −17 dB, 12 dB below any other mLink signal, and well below what you can see on a waterfall.
  • It is slow on purpose. One frame takes 10.24 seconds and carries 11 bytes. The first frame alone is a complete alert. Up to 14 more add who is calling and what is wrong, 13 characters each.
  • It repeats. A call goes out again about every 75 to 90 seconds until someone acknowledges it or the operator cancels it. It survives a restart of the station software.
  • It leaves room for an answer. After each pass the calling station listens for at least 15 seconds.
  • It does not wait for anyone. Distress traffic ignores reservations, and sending a call cuts off whatever else the station was transmitting; that message is kept in Drafts. No setting, password or missing field can stop a call being sent. An empty call is still a valid call.

10.1 When a station hears one

  • It alerts its operator.
  • It repeats the call in the announce strip after a short random wait, and stands down if another station does so first.
  • A gateway puts the call in a mailbox watched for distress calls, so a person with a telephone can act on it.
  • An operator can acknowledge it. The acknowledgment goes out twice: with contact details in the announce strip, and as one frame in the emergency strip, timed for the moment the caller starts listening, so the answer reaches as deep as the call did.

Full manual: "The emergency modem", "Emergency".

Chapter 11

Clusters: one station, several radios

A station can be more than one radio. Several computers, each running the daemon with its own radio on its own band, join into a cluster that shares one callsign and behaves as one station.

  • The link between members is a network, a LAN or the internet. It is never the radio. Up to 16 members.
  • Everything heard is shared. A station heard on 40 m by one member appears in the station list of every member, with the band it was heard on.
  • Traffic crosses bands. A message for a station that only the 80 m member hears is carried over the link and sent from the 80 m radio.
  • One operator view. An operator's client connects to any member and works the whole station. Accounts, passwords and the mail identity are the same everywhere.
  • Mail is protected against losing a member. A member with internet access holds a copy of the others' unsent mail and takes it over if its owner has been silent for three minutes.
  • Each radio still decides for itself. When to transmit on a band is decided only from what that band's radio hears.

Joining takes a label, the address of one existing member, and approval by an administrator who compares two key fingerprints.

Full manual: "Clusters".

Chapter 12

Also built in

12.1 Stations find each other

A new station listens for ten minutes before it sends anything. If it hears other stations it joins in; if it hears nobody it asks whether anyone can hear it. After that it sends a short beacon now and then, less often on a crowded frequency. Every station keeps a list of who it has heard and how strongly, and stations pass each other short notes about who hears whom, which builds a map of the links on the frequency.

12.2 Position and maps

Every station is placed on a world map from its grid square, or exactly with a GPS. Stations can send position reports that leave a trail, and operators can share map objects such as search areas and routes. A message can be addressed to "any station in this area".

12.3 Internet data over the air

A station without internet can ask for data such as a weather report. A station with internet fetches it and sends back a compact answer.

12.4 Operators and roles

Several operators can work one station at once, each with an account. Three roles (operator, technician, administrator) decide who may change the station's settings. Sending a distress call is never restricted.

12.5 Identity and encryption

mLink encrypts with AES-256-GCM. Two stations that each hold a signed certificate agree a key without sending anything secret, or a group shares one key in advance. A message is compressed first and then sealed. Encryption is for services whose rules allow it, such as MARS and SHARES; amateur rules forbid hiding what a message says, so it stays off there unless keys are loaded and it is asked for per message. Separately, and on every service, a mail carries a short proof of its author that the mailserver checks. Distress calls are never encrypted.

12.6 FM

FmLink is mLink for FM voice radios and repeaters. It works today and has been run on the air between two inexpensive handhelds keyed by VOX, the slowest way a radio can be keyed. Radios the computer controls directly will run a faster tier, and that is where the work is going. The cheap handheld is supported because so many people own one, not because it is a good way to run mLink.

Full manual: "Finding each other", "Position and maps", "Internet sources", "Accounts and roles", "Security".

Chapter 13

Getting on the air

13.1 What you need

  • An SSB transceiver of the kind used for FT8 or JS8, with filters that pass audio from about 115 to 2,900 Hz.
  • A sound interface between the radio and the computer: a SignaLink-type or Digirig-type box, or a radio with a sound card built in.
  • A way to key the transmitter: a serial control line, the radio's CAT control, or VOX. The first two are faster.
  • A Linux computer for the station daemon. The client runs on Linux, on Windows and in any web browser, and can be on another machine.

13.2 First steps

  1. Download the package, one file that holds the daemon, the client and the web client, and run it. It opens the client: 
    chmod +x mLink-x86_64.AppImage
./mLink-x86_64.AppImage
  2. In the client, enter your callsign and grid square, choose the sound device and the keying method, and set the transmit audio level so the radio shows no ALC action.
  3. Tune to a frequency where mLink stations are active. Your station listens for ten minutes before it transmits by itself.
  4. Open the Stations window to see who you hear. Open Messages, write to one of them, and press Send.

13.3 What to expect

  • A sent message may sit in the outbox for a few seconds, sometimes a minute, while the station waits for a free moment.
  • Deferred means the station still has the message and will try again. Failed means it has stopped, and says why.
  • Your station also works for others: it relays, repeats distress calls and, with internet access, carries mail. You can limit this in the settings.
  • You can close the client. The station keeps running.

Full manual: "Installing", "Operator guide", "Outbox and waiting".

Chapter 14

How demanding is it?

One long test on the simulated air put 200 stations on one 20 m frequency for six hours: a search-and-rescue net of 15, an evening net of 30, and 155 others ranging from chatty to silent, with 123 of the 200 acting as mail gateways. Each station heard about 177 of the others, some station was transmitting about four fifths of the time, and nothing coordinated them except the two nets. The test was run twice with the same script, and the two runs differ; where they do, both figures are given.

  • It carries a busy frequency. Nearly everything short arrives within minutes; mail that waits for a gateway or for the addressee to come on the air takes tens of minutes.
  • What size costs is repeats, not the exchange. One typed line, acknowledged, costs 9.2 s of air with 28 stations on or with 200. With 200, a third of the text frames are repeats of text that was lost under another station's transmission, often from a station out of the sender's range.
  • Weak spots under load, and their causes: a quarter to two fifths of the messages that asked for another station's help got no offer, mostly because a helper must have heard the addressee within the last two minutes and quiet stations identify themselves only once an hour; a large casual net's roll calls reached about one member in four or five, mostly because members had not yet learned their own number from the roster, which arrives three entries at a time; and about a hundred gateways answer one station's transmission in four to eight shared moments, so four fifths of "you have mail" announcements are repeats.
  • Also found: a gateway overhearing a mail carried by a relay names the relay as the author, and after five such deposits the mailserver refuses that station's deposits for ten minutes, its own mail included; and the largest message (46,000 bytes) arrived in one run of the two, since a message that size is only tried on a measured link.
  • The figures are from a script. The text was generated, so real mail will compress differently, and the simulated operators did not react to what they read.
  • On the computer: a station program on that busy frequency used about 3 % of one processor core and 50 MB of memory, before sound card, rig control and the client.
